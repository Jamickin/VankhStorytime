# Chapter 1.2.2: The Mechanic

"Everything flows, out and in; everything has its tides; all things rise and fall; the pendulum swing manifests in everything."

---

The nutrition dispensary's front panel offered seventeen varieties of compressed protein supplement and a queue of zero customers. [[Pro]] pressed the contact plate, stated his identifier, and was through the back door before anyone in the concourse glanced his way.

[[Mechanic]] [[Augi]] was elbow-deep in a problem.

The workbench held a disassembled neural array — not a repair job, he saw immediately, but a full rebuild. Someone's entire augmentation stack had been laid out in ordered rows on the clean surface, each component tagged with a color-coded identifier. [[Augi]] worked by touch as much as sight, her modified hands navigating the components with a precision that no scanning equipment could have equaled. She had been designed — had designed herself — for exactly this kind of work, iteration by iteration, modification accreted over decades until the original woman was an archaeological layer somewhere beneath the surface.

"I'm going to need you," she said, without looking up.

"I have a delivery to make first."

"Your mother's prescription is already packaged." She tilted her head toward a sealed tray at the edge of the bench. "The compound cost more this cycle. Your payment chip will cover it." She lifted a component with two fingers and held it to the light — a neural bridge housing, its contacts filmed with something that needed cleaning before reinstallation. "Sit. I need a second pair of hands with calibrated pressure. Your tolerance ratings are in your file."

[[Pro]] set down his tool bag, put the pharmaceutical pack in the inner pocket where it wouldn't get jostled, and settled onto the work stool. "What is it?"

"Neural array rebuild. Full stack replacement, with the original architecture preserved in parallel." She passed him the bridge housing and a cleaning tool calibrated to micron tolerances. "The client needs to pass the [[Helix Habitat]] anti-augmentation screen without removing their actual augmentations. The new shell reads as organic. The original architecture routes beneath it."

"Illicit," he said.

"They would say survival." She picked up the next component. "[[Helix Habitat]]'s screen was built to detect standard augmentation profiles. I have a nonstandard approach." She said it without pride. It was simply a description of a problem and its solution.

He began cleaning the bridge housing in the pattern she had indicated — light pressure, circular, the contaminating film lifting in thin grey sheets that he transferred to a waste cloth. They worked in silence for a time, the sounds of the concourse muffled to a background frequency behind the dispensary's insulated walls.

"You have [[Vankh proximity]] hours logged," [[Augi]] said. It was not a question. She had his file.

"Eight [[Vankh-Sett]]s of coupling work. Twelve proximity incidents, standard classification." He passed the housing back. "Why?"

She did not answer directly. She laid the housing into a recess in the array's base frame, tested the contact fit, and reached for the next piece.

"There is a pattern in the proximity incident reports," she said. "Corporate files them as [[Dilation Hysteria]]. Workers who experienced temporal anomalies while using standard breathing near a sphere. Standard breathing means standard protocol — the Left-Right-Left-Left-Right pattern. Near a [[Vankh sphere]] that protocol is suppressed for a reason. Anyone who used it anyway and survived long enough to file a report described, in each case, a perceptual event they could not account for."

[[Pro]] held the cleaning tool still. "Described it how?"

"That is the interesting part." [[Augi]] turned a component over, peering at its underside with eyes that had been modified for exactly this kind of inspection. "They described it differently. Each used their own terms, their own frame of reference. Temporal slippage, parallel resonance, the sensation of being perceived, an awareness that the space around the sphere was in some way inhabited." She set the component down and looked at him directly for the first time since he had arrived. "Corporate reads inconsistent descriptions as evidence of psychological disruption. Hallucination. Dilation Hysteria. The reports all end the same way: worker reassigned, incident classified, case closed." A pause. "But the core of each description is consistent. Every one of them perceived something that was also perceiving them."

The hum of the recyclers ran beneath everything — the station's constant breath, the atmosphere cycling in and out of this sealed room the same as everywhere else.

"They all saw the same thing," [[Pro]] said.

"They experienced the same thing. Through very different perceptual filters, in very different terms. But the phenomenology — the shape of what happened — is identical across fourteen reports spanning six [[Vankh-Sett]]s." She picked up the next component. "I have been collecting these for a long time. It is what I do with information I cannot use but cannot discard."

"What do the reports say happened to the workers?"

"The reassignments are uniform. Proximity work, terminated. No further Vankh-adjacent assignments permitted. For their safety." She said the last two words with an evenness that let him decide what he thought of them. "None of them ever filed a second incident report. Whether because they stopped having experiences, or because they stopped reporting them, the records do not say."

[[Pro]] looked at the array on the bench — someone's complete cognitive architecture, laid out in pieces, being rebuilt into something that could pass as what it was not. He thought about the secondary harmonic he had heard under the [[Vankh pulse]] during transit. The pattern that had built behind his sternum like a second heartbeat. The thing that had seemed, at the edge of perception, to respond when he focused on it.

"The reference codes," he said. "The reports are accessible?"

"Three of them are accessible at your clearance. Six are classified above it. Five are missing entirely — the archive records their existence but returns no data." [[Augi]] laid the component in place and took a small tool from a magnetic strip on the bench's edge. "I am going to give you the reference codes for the three accessible ones. And I am going to give you the reference numbers for the six classified ones." She did not look at him. "What you do with the latter is not my concern."

She set the tool down and took a data chip from the drawer beneath the bench — small, matte black, standard corporate format. She held it out without ceremony.

He took it.

"I want to be precise about what I am not telling you," [[Augi]] said. "I am not telling you that the [[Prophet]] was right. I am not telling you that the spheres are conscious. I am not drawing conclusions from these reports, because conclusions drawn from patterns I cannot test are stories, not knowledge." She went back to the array. "I am telling you that fourteen people experienced something coherent and consistent, that the corporate system has a strong institutional interest in classifying that experience as pathology, and that three of the reports are available to someone with your access codes. The rest is your own business."

[[Pro]] turned the chip over in his fingers. It was lighter than the pharmaceutical pack, and carried more weight.

He set it in the inner pocket beside his mother's medicine.

They worked for another hour — him cleaning, holding, aligning components on instruction; [[Augi]] fitting, testing, adjusting with a tactile fluency that made his own enhanced hands feel approximate. She did not return to the subject of the reports. Neither did he. There was a quality to her silence that suggested the topic was not closed, only set aside in the way a careful technician sets aside a component that requires more thought before reinstallation.

When the array was complete — or as complete as this session would take it — he helped her tray the components in the order she specified. His hands remembered the sequence without being told twice.

"Come back when the prescription needs renewing," she said. "Or when you have questions about what you read."

He picked up his tool bag.

"If you had to guess," he said, "what were they seeing?"

[[Augi]] was quiet for a long moment. The recyclers breathed around them.

"I don't guess," she said. "It's a bad habit in this work." She turned to the next task on her bench. "But I think that whatever is at the other end of the [[Vankh pulse]] is aware of the same boundary condition from its side that we are barely beginning to notice from ours."

[[Pro]] left through the dispensary door and stepped back into the noise of the concourse.

He did not look up the reference codes that day. He walked to the public transit hub, found a seat in the blue section, and sat with his tool bag between his feet and the chip in his pocket and his mother's medicine in his other pocket, and let the station's hum settle into the background where it lived.

There would be time. He had always worked better when he let the data sit before he touched it.
