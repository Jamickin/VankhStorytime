# Chapter 2.1.1: The Hull

"As above, so below; as below, so above."

---

The captain said the atmosphere system was a secondary issue. [[Pro]] had heard that phrase before — always from people who meant it was someone else's problem until it became a crisis, at which point it became everyone's problem simultaneously and usually at the worst possible moment.

He took the work order anyway. His other contracts were filed. He needed the hours.

The cargo hold of the independent vessel occupied the lower third of the ship's hull, separated from the passenger and crew sections by two sets of pressure doors and an access ladder that had been welded at an angle inconsistent with the original schematics — a later modification, done by someone working in a hurry with the wrong grade of alloy. [[Pro]] noted it without remarking on it. Ships accreted their histories this way. Every improvised repair was a sentence in a story the vessel told about its own life.

The atmosphere system fault was genuine and minor: a recirculation valve that had developed a partial block from particulate buildup. He cleaned it, tested the pressure differential, logged the fix. Sixteen minutes from entering the hold to completing the repair notation in his station tablet.

That was when he saw the crate.

It sat in the far corner of the hold, against the bulkhead. Not improperly stowed — it was secured to the deck with standard cargo strapping in good condition. What was improper was the gap along its upper seal, where the case material had separated under repeated vibrational stress. The seam ran the full length of the lid, and the gap was wide enough for his scanner to register what was inside without him doing anything he hadn't already done in the normal course of a hull inspection.

He stood still for a moment.

The manifest tag on the crate's exterior read: _ARCHIVAL MATERIALS — PRIVATE COLLECTION — ORIGIN: [[Helix Habitat]]. DESTINATION: CICILLIAN STATION SEVEN-OMEGA. CONSIGNEE: PRIVATE._

He looked at the gap.

His scanner was already in his hand. He had been using it for the atmosphere repair. He was still, technically, in the performance of his work.

[[Pro]] ran the fastest materials scan he had ever executed — thirty-eight seconds, the scanner angled into the gap, reading the surfaces of whatever was inside. He did not reach in. He did not touch the seal. He stood at the crate with his scanner at his side, pointed into the gap, and read the data as it returned.

Archive chips. A dozen, maybe more, in protective cases. The cases were labeled in a pre-Consolidation format that he recognized from his mother's research materials — the old [[Helix Habitat]] administrative standard, before the corporate information systems had standardized notation across all stations. His mother had spent two decades learning to read that format.

He could see two labels clearly from the gap. He captured them in his scanner's image memory and kept going:

_POPULATION REGISTRY: PRE-CONSOLIDATION CYCLE 7, HELIX SYSTEM._

_DEMOGRAPHIC BASELINE RECORDS, HELIX SYSTEM, CYCLES 1-3._

His mother's crystal held demographic archives. She had spent years building a record of population irregularities — numbers that didn't change, birthrates with no variation, age distributions that never aged. He had taken the crystal from her hands as she lay in the clinic bed, her voice thin, her eyes too bright. He had not yet opened it. He had not been ready.

The same kind of records. Going somewhere else. Being preserved, or being controlled, or being removed from the place where someone might use them to ask the right questions — he couldn't tell which from a shipping label and thirty-eight seconds of scanner time.

[[VVVVVANKH!]]

The pulse rolled through the hold and every archive chip in the crate rattled in its case. [[Pro]] stepped back from the crate, dropped his scanner into his tool bag with a motion that looked, if anyone was watching, like a technician wrapping up a job, and turned toward the exit ladder.

"Technician P-R-0-4-7-9."

[[Lin]] stood at the hold's entrance. She had come down the access ladder without noise — or he had been too focused on the crate to hear her. She wore the same corporate administrative attire he had first seen her in at the berth, and the mathematical enhancement patterns under her skin were active, running through calculations he could not see the shape of. Her presence in the hold at this precise moment did not feel like coincidence, and he did not insult either of them by treating it as one.

"Supervisor," he said.

"The atmosphere system fault?" Her voice had the same quality as her attention: precise, without warmth, not hostile.

"Cleared. Recirculation valve block. Logged and filed." He closed his tool bag. "The fault was in the secondary return line, not the primary. It would have degraded slowly."

"I've reviewed your notation." She looked at the crate. She did not look at him. "Your work order covers the atmosphere system. Cargo inspection is not within your authorized scope on this contract."

"I was aware of that."

"I'm noting your presence in cargo in the work record." She met his eyes. "For your awareness."

"Understood."

She said nothing more about it. He climbed the access ladder first and she followed, and they emerged into the service corridor together, and she peeled off toward the corporate administrative level without further comment. No reprimand. No accusation. An observation noted, filed, and placed in his record where it would remain, available to anyone who needed it.

He stood in the service corridor and felt the distance between the cargo hold below him and the private collector's destination, the last [[Cicillian]] station at the far edge of the network.

Whoever had those records would be the only person, outside that crate, who had access to pre-Consolidation demographic baselines for the Helix system. Whatever questions those records could answer, only one private collector would be positioned to ask them.

Or prevent them from being asked.

His mother had built her archive over twenty years, from fragments and partial records and data sources that were disappearing, one by one, as the Church of Purity extended its reach and corporate systems standardized what was preserved and what was not. She had not told him why she was building it. She had only said: _the records don't match. The numbers don't work the way populations work._

He thought about that crate in the hold below him. He thought about where it was going. He thought about the two labels he had captured in his scanner's memory, and the ten or twelve others he had not been able to read.

He began walking toward the public concourse, and did not yet know what to do with what he had seen.
