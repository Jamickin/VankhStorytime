# Chapter 2.1.2: The Independent

"As above, so below; as below, so above."

---

"I remember you," the captain said.

He was waiting at the mouth of the maintenance tunnel when [[Pro]] emerged from the berth inspection, the captain's crystalline ancestry patterns faint beneath weathered skin. The modifications had settled deep over the years, the way modifications did on people who had worked the same routes long enough to become part of the machinery. "You're the one who kept my coupling from killing everyone on the shuttle. I remember faces."

[[Pro]] stopped. He had time before the report to [[Lin]] was due. Not much time, but some. "And?"

"I have a job. Private client. Coupling evaluation on a vessel that can't go to corporate service." The captain read his expression accurately. "Nothing illegal in the evaluation itself. The client has reasons for needing discretion. You don't have to know what they are."

"Where is the client?"

The captain tilted his head toward the orange section of the ring. The [[Purist]] enclave. [[Pro]] looked at his corporate badge — visible, unambiguous, the exact wrong thing to be wearing in the orange concourses.

He went anyway.

The orange section announced itself by texture before it announced itself by color. The smooth polymer floors of the [[Cicillian]] concourses gave way to poured stone composite — deliberately rough, the kind of surface that registered underfoot as principled rather than unfinished. No holographic advertisements here. No commercial overlay for augmented eyes to read. Physical signage in large print, the text in the old [[Purist]] script that corporate standardization had never managed to fully displace. He kept his badge visible — the alternative was to conceal it, which would have been worse — and moved quickly, making no eye contact with the clusters of unaugmented residents who tracked his passage with the particular attention of people who have built their lives around the distinction between themselves and what he represented.

The berth the captain had directed him to was in a cross-section of the ring where the [[Purist]] enclaves and the independent additions overlapped — a zone of negotiated informality, where the architecture reflected compromise rather than principle. Here a rough stone facade sat bolted to a polymer frame. There a corporate-standard airlock had been retrofitted with a manual release override. Everything worked. Nothing agreed.

The man waiting beside the vessel was retired in the way that serious professionals became retired — not diminished, but redirected. The work had settled into him rather than departing. He was old enough that his Cicillian modifications had been installed under pre-standardization protocols, the neural interfaces showing the slightly different contact geometry of an earlier generation of enhancement work. He wore no corporate insignia. The twenty-[[Vankh-Sett]]s since he had left [[Cicillian Industries]] were visible in the absence, the way the shape of a tool can sometimes be read in what it leaves behind.

"Ren," the captain said. "The technician I mentioned."

The old engineer — Ren — extended a hand. His grip was engineer's grip, measuring.

"The coupling first," [[Pro]] said. "Then we talk."

The coupling was original-spec. He knew it before his scanner confirmed it: the weight distribution of the housing was different, the alloy composition pre-standardization, the contact geometry from an era before corporate integration had flattened all the variation out of [[Vankh]]-harnessing equipment. He had read about this architecture in maintenance manuals that [[Cicillian]] no longer circulated. He had never worked on a physical example.

He moved slowly, taking his time, letting his scanner build a complete picture. The coupling was in good condition — better than most contemporary hardware he serviced. Whoever had maintained it knew what they were doing and had been doing it for a long time. The pre-standardization architecture had tolerances corporate engineers had since abandoned in favor of efficiency. Those tolerances made the equipment harder to manufacture and harder to certify. They also made it quieter, in the harmonic sense — less interference with the [[Vankh pulse]]'s subtler frequencies.

Better, he thought, for listening.

"Twenty [[Vankh-Sett]]s," Ren said, watching him work. "I ran passive scans the entire time. Secondary harmonic surveys, mostly. You know what a secondary harmonic survey is?"

[[Pro]] kept his eyes on the coupling. "I've been hearing one since my last transit."

That stopped Ren for a moment. He recovered. "Then you know what I was listening for."

"Show me what you have."

Ren's scan archive was stored on a data chip so old that [[Pro]]'s reader had to run a compatibility protocol before the format resolved. When it did, the data was dense — twenty [[Vankh-Sett]]s of secondary harmonic measurements, taken from multiple positions across the shipping routes Ren had worked during his corporate years and continued after his departure. The archive was organized with an engineer's precision: date, position, pulse cycle, secondary deviation, environmental variables. Twenty years of a man listening to something no one had told him to listen for and recording what he heard.

The pattern was not noise.

[[Pro]] turned through the data slowly, and the shape of it assembled itself in his mind the way a coupling's fault signature assembled itself — not all at once, but progressively, each reading adding to the picture until the picture was undeniable. The secondary harmonic was consistent across all measurements. Not random. Not chaotic. A signal with a detectable structure and a detectable period.

And the anomaly.

It appeared first in the seventeenth page of data and then again four pages later and again after that, at intervals that were not quite regular but were not random. A shift in the secondary harmonic's baseline. Small — within measurement error for most instruments. Ren's pre-standardization equipment had the tolerance range to detect it. Corporate standard gear would have classified it as noise and filtered it out.

Each time the baseline shifted, it shifted in the same direction, by approximately the same degree, and then stabilized at the new value. And some later cycle it would shift back.

"Scheduled," [[Pro]] said.

"Maintenance," Ren said. "I concluded that in my third [[Vankh-Sett]] of observation. Something performs adjustments to the secondary harmonic on a schedule I cannot predict but can observe in hindsight. The adjustments are corrective — the harmonic is being kept within a range. Whatever is at the other end of that signal has an intended operating parameter, and when it drifts, something brings it back."

[[VVVVVANKH!]]

The pulse came through the berth and the coupling sang with it — the pre-standardization architecture resonating differently than any equipment [[Pro]] had serviced in eight years, cleaner, more complete, as if the old design had been built to hear something the new one had been built to ignore. Under the pulse, the secondary harmonic was present and distinct, and for a moment he had the vertiginous sensation that it had always been there and that he had only recently learned to perceive it.

"The pulse is not natural," [[Pro]] said.

"I came to that conclusion in my seventh [[Vankh-Sett]]." Ren spoke without drama. "Natural systems don't hold this kind of precision. Natural systems drift. Whatever the [[Vankh spheres]] are, they don't drift. And whatever maintains them doesn't drift either." He took the data chip from [[Pro]]'s reader and looked at it for a moment. "I stopped looking because I decided I didn't want to know what that meant." He held it out. "Take it."

[[Pro]] took the chip. It was heavier than data chips had any reason to be.

"What did it mean to you?" he asked. "When you were still looking."

Ren was quiet for a long moment. In the corridor outside the berth, the station moved around them — the sounds of cargo being loaded and unloaded, the distant pulse synchronization that kept every system on [[Nexus Station]] breathing in alignment with the network.

"It meant," Ren said at last, "that the [[Vankh pulse]] is not something that happened to the universe. It's something being done to it. Actively. By something with a schedule and a method and a tolerance range for error." He picked up his tools and began the ritual process of closing up after a job — the careful stowing of equipment that people who work alone develop as a meditative practice. "I stopped because I am old, and I have had a good life, and I did not want to end it asking questions whose answers would make everything I had done feel like someone else's theater."

He looked at [[Pro]].

"You're not old yet."

[[Pro]] put the data chip in his tool bag, beside his mother's medicine and the pharmaceutical records and the scanner readings from the hull and everything else he had been accumulating in the way he accumulated anomalies: without urgency, without a plan, with the technician's faith that at some point the data would tell him what it meant.

The [[Vankh pulse]] was not something that happened to the universe.

It was something being done to it.

He walked back through the orange concourses with his badge visible and his eyes forward, and let the thought sit in him the way a new reading sat while he decided what it meant.
