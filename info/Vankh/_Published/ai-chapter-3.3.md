# Chapter 3.3: Sector Seven

"Nothing rests; everything moves; everything vibrates; everything is in motion; everything vibrates."

---

Relay Station One had been built before the second expansion, when proximity maintenance near the [[Vankh spheres]] was conducted by researchers who considered the possibility of [[FTL travel]] a theoretical convenience rather than an infrastructure requirement. The original installation showed it: coupling architectures designed to be accessed one-handed because the other hand was always on a notepad, monitoring equipment that gave analogue readouts alongside digital ones because nobody had decided yet which to trust, mounting brackets that accommodated seventeen different tool systems because the standard hadn't been established and wouldn't be for another thirty Vankh-Setts.

[[Pro]] had been working in the original coupling bay for three hours when the interface presented him with an error sequence he had never encountered before.

The sequence was not in the corporate documentation. It was not in the specialized handbook his training authority had issued for the assignment. It was not in the eleven years of accumulated working knowledge he carried in muscle memory and neural interface cache. It was a fault reading specific to the original harnessing equipment — the technology that had been built not to use the [[Vankh pulse]], but to understand it. The kind of system that expected its operator to trace harmonic frequencies by hand, lining up resonance profiles the way his mother had described lining up archival dates: following the pattern until the discrepancy revealed itself.

He traced it. His breathing followed his attention without his noticing.

*Left lung. Right lung. Left lung. Left lung. Right lung.*

The forward viewport of the original coupling bay looked directly at the sphere.

The third Vankh was perfect and absolutely black and occupied approximately thirty degrees of his peripheral vision even at seventeen kilometers. The [[Vankh pulse]] moved through the station at intervals that vibrated in his chest cavity rather than his ears — a frequency below sound that he had always felt without having a name for it. The secondary harmonic was present beneath it, consistent, familiar now as his own pulse.

Then they were there.

They had not moved into view. They had simply been there when he looked — three of them at the sphere's surface, where the station's forward viewport met his peripheral awareness, tall and slender, their skin absorbing the viewport's ambient light with the same completeness as the sphere itself. No features he could read from seventeen kilometers. Except the eyes, which were present with a clarity that didn't correspond to the distance.

They were looking at him.

Not at the station. The station had been here for generations and they had not, presumably, been looking at it. At him. The same quality of attention he had felt through the aperture on the shuttle from the [[Helix Habitat]] — but direct now, without the mediation of transit space, and carrying something with it that was not communication in any framework he had and was not quite not-communication either.

One of them was closer to the viewport than the others. Or the viewport had not moved and it had. It transmitted something directly — not through his interfaces, not through his diagnostic equipment — through the same pathway that his mother's secondary harmonic had been mapping for the last two Parts of his life.

Old. Patient. It had been carrying this for a very long time.

He forced the reverse breathing back.

*Right lung. Left lung. Left lung. Right lung. Right lung.*

The Vankh proximity protocol — desynchronize, counter-rhythm, go below the threshold where the [[Vankh pulse]] reaches. His neural interfaces protested the shift, recalibrating. The station's monitoring equipment registered his biometric deviation in its automatic logging, a thirty-second window of anomalous respiratory pattern recorded without judgment into the station telemetry that would upload to the corporate maintenance network at the end of his shift.

The three figures at the sphere's surface held in the viewport for a long beat after his breathing shifted. Long enough that he understood the delay was intentional — they wanted to be seen — and then the reversal of his breathing displaced them below whatever threshold they required, and the sphere was alone in the viewport again, as it had presumably been every day of the station's existence in the official record.

He stood in the coupling bay with his hands still on the original harnessing equipment and the error sequence still unresolved on the interface, and understood several things at once:

That the corporate reverse breathing protocol was not a radiation safety measure.

That every Vankh-proximity technician who had ever worked here had been trained specifically so as not to see what he had just seen.

That whatever had just been transmitted to him directly was now part of him in a way that the station's telemetry would not capture.

And that the telemetry had captured everything else.

He looked at his diagnostic equipment — the non-standard tools spread across his maintenance cloth, enhanced sensitivity, broader spectrum, pattern-recognition tuned for configurations no corporate manual acknowledged.
